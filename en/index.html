A simple formula from mental training

Performance=potential− interference

In mental training, performance is often described with a simple formula. Potential is the competence, experience and skills we have. Interference is whatever makes them harder to use. For individuals, it is often doubt, pressure or an inner voice telling you that you are not good enough.

The formula is originally about the individual, but it is a useful picture for teams too. In technology organisations, the potential is often high. At the same time, the interference can be considerable, and much of it is social: the fear of being judged, the shame of a mistake, or uncertainty about what is safe to say. It does not only sit within the individual, but also in decision paths, relationships and culture.

This means performance can improve without the potential changing. So the fastest route to better delivery is not always more people, but less standing in the way of the people who are already there.

The formula is taken from Myhre and Aas (2025), Prestasjonsledelse (Performance leadership).

For leaders, developers and leadership teams

Where to begin

The texts can be read in order, but they land a little differently depending on where you stand.

If you lead a team

Start with article 2 and 3, then read article 6 on the leader’s paradox. Look for where decisions pile up with one person. And notice how you react the next time someone says something is not going to plan. That reaction teaches the team more than any values poster.

If you are a developer

Start with article 3, 4 and 7. Think of the last code review where you held back a question, or spent an extra hour making something perfect before anyone else got to see it. What would have made it easier to share earlier? If you recognise yourself, the deep dive on inner dialogue offers concrete tools.

If you are on a leadership team

Start with article 1 and 5, then read article 6 on what it takes for teams to lead themselves. The deep dive on regulation capacity goes further. A key point there is that the level of tension, the norms around mistakes and the room for learning are largely set at the top. That cannot be delegated to HR or to the teams.

Deep dives

Where theory meets practice.

Two papers from my mental trainer education at Raw Performance. They are longer and more theoretical than the articles, for those who want to go deeper.

Mental trainer · level 22026

Psychological safety as regulation capacity

An analysis of emotional activation and regulation in performance-oriented knowledge organisations

Amy Edmondson describes psychological safety as an interpersonal climate. The paper asks what it is about safety that actually affects performance, and argues that it reduces the social threat that makes it hard to think clearly in code reviews, decision meetings and after production incidents. It also discusses the pitfalls, including the risk that regulation becomes yet another performance demand on the individual.

Read the paperapprox. 35 minutes→
Mental trainer · level 12024

Inner dialogue

How can we use inner dialogue to overcome impostor syndrome?

A practical walkthrough of how thought patterns can make competent people doubt their own competence. The paper presents concrete tools, such as thought records, the STOP method and ABC-E, with examples from working life: asking for help, presenting to management and leading an important client meeting.

About me

From software development to people and organisations.

I have a background in software development, and have since worked with team coordination, leadership and HR in knowledge-intensive IT organisations. Today I lead HR at Liflig and am part of the leadership team.

I trained as a mental trainer at Raw Performance. It has given me a language for how thoughts, feelings and relationships affect what people achieve, not just on the sports field, but in an architecture discussion too.

What I write grows out of the meeting between those two worlds: between professional strength and relational safety, between individual and system, and between good intentions and actual impact.

  • SafetyThe foundation. Safety is not about the absence of demands or challenges, but about an environment where there is room to be human, also when you are unsure or make mistakes. That makes it easier to be honest, ask questions and show vulnerability.
  • CourageA precondition for growth. Growth often happens when we dare to stay with discomfort, try something new or move beyond the familiar. With safety around us, that courage becomes easier to find.
  • AuthenticityMeeting others genuinely and sincerely: being present, listening actively and being open to different perspectives and experiences. When we meet each other with honesty and curiosity, more people dare to be themselves.

My intention is to help create safety, so that people dare to be themselves and dare to grow.
