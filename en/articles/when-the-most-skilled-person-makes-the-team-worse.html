When competence becomes value · part 2

When the most skilled person makes the team worse

Camilla KarudApprox. 4 minute read

Some people can solve problems that almost nobody else in the team can. They spot risks early, have deep expertise and deliver high quality. That makes them valuable. But there is a question we ask less often when we assess our strongest professionals: What happens to the performance of the people around them?

This article is part of the series “When competence becomes value”, which explores the people, relationships and systems behind good technology delivery.

Individual performance is not the same as team performance

An expert can create great value through what they deliver themselves. But a team is a system, and the way one person works affects how others work. If every important decision has to be checked by the same person, others may gradually become more concerned with finding the expected answer than with developing their own judgement.

A composite example

In one team, a very experienced developer held a great deal of domain knowledge. They were thorough and cared about quality. Over time, however, the others began clearing more and more decisions with them before moving on. Nobody had decided it should be that way; the team had simply learned that it was safer to ask first. It could easily be read as a lack of independence in the rest of the team. But what if some of that independence had actually been trained away?

When the relationship disturbs performance

Mental training works with the idea that the capacity a person has and what they manage to express in a given situation are not always the same. Pressure and self-focus can affect attention and performance. Research on performance under pressure shows precisely that attentional processes can be part of the explanation when performance drops in demanding situations.

At work, a similar mechanism can be at play in relationships. You know what you think, but start doubting yourself when one particular person challenges you. You have the mandate, but feel the need to check first. You spend more capacity on how to phrase things than on the problem itself. Here we use the term relational interference to describe mental and emotional disturbances in performance that arise or are amplified in interaction with others.

The invisible cost

The team can still deliver. That is exactly what makes this hard to detect. Work is double-checked, decisions are postponed and ideas are only shared once they are hard to criticise. The first cost is not necessarily lower output. It can be a higher mental cost of delivering the same.

From expert to capacity builder

This does not mean strong professionals should become less clear. Teams need people who set high standards, spot risks and challenge weak solutions. But the truly strong professional also manages to increase the capacity around them: asking questions before giving the answer, sharing context, accepting other sound solutions and gradually reducing support as others develop.

When we put teams together, individual competence is therefore only one part of the picture. It matters just as much whether people share knowledge, build independence and make competence something the team owns rather than one person.

A strong team player is judged not only by what they can deliver, but by what the people around them become able to deliver.
