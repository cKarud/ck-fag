When competence becomes value · part 4

You have the competence. But do you get to use it?

Camilla KarudApprox. 4 minute read

Organisations spend considerable resources on competence. We recruit skilled people, bring in specialists and build professional communities. But there is a difference between having competence and having access to it. Competence only creates value when people actually use it.

This article is part of the series “When competence becomes value”, which explores the people, relationships and systems behind good technology delivery.

The competence loss that doesn’t show up in the accounts

It is easy to spot the competence you lack. It is harder to spot the competence you already have but don’t get to use. A developer sees a risk but says nothing. A new hire has a different perspective but waits until they have understood “how we do things here”. An experienced colleague spends a disproportionate amount of time checking their own work before anyone else gets to see it.

A composite example

A competent employee was given responsibility for an area they were well equipped to handle. Even so, they spent more and more time preparing and double-checking, and waited a long time before asking for input. From the outside, it looked like a strong sense of responsibility. Only when the way of working was examined more closely did it become clear that much of the capacity was going into reducing the risk of appearing inadequate.

Competence is potential – performance happens in the situation

Mental training is interesting precisely in the gap between what we can do and what we manage to express. Self-regulation, attention and how we assess our own mastery affect our actions. Albert Bandura’s work on self-efficacy and human agency also emphasises that people’s assessment of their own capacity is part of how they act, regulate themselves and take control in situations.

For some, the uncertainty can resemble what is often called impostor feelings: a persistent sense of not being as competent as others think. You may then wait too long before asking for help, over-prepare, or avoid tasks where the risk of failing is visible. From the outside it can look like thoroughness. On the inside, a lot of capacity can go into managing your own uncertainty.

But this is not only the individual’s responsibility

There is an important trap here. If we see the problem as purely individual, the solution quickly becomes that the person needs to become more confident or more robust. Mental tools can help, but we also have to ask: What is the person trying to regulate themselves through?

How does the team react when someone asks a basic question? Can a less experienced colleague challenge a senior? What happens to the person who says the plan might not hold? People quickly learn what pays to show, and what pays to hold back.

From staffing to available competence

When we put together teams at Liflig, we therefore don’t only look at which roles and technologies need to be represented. We also look at how people work together. A strong team consists of people who share knowledge, ask for help, challenge each other and help more people take independent responsibility.

The question is not only whether we have the right people, but whether we have created an environment where they actually get to use what they know.
