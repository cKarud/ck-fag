When competence becomes value · part 1

Why skilled technology teams don’t always create enough value

Camilla KarudApprox. 4 minute read

Many organisations invest heavily in technology. They hire skilled developers, bring in specialists and build teams with solid experience. Yet development moves more slowly than hoped, priorities become difficult, and it can be unclear whether the investments are creating the effect people were hoping for.

It is then tempting to ask: Do we need more developers? Sometimes the answer is yes. But before increasing capacity, it is worth asking a different question: What is stopping the people we already have from creating more value?

This article is part of the series “When competence becomes value”, which explores the people, relationships and systems behind good technology delivery.

Capacity is more than headcount

A technology team’s capacity is not determined only by how many people are in it, or how strong their CVs are. It is shaped by the system around the team: Do people understand which problem they are solving? Do they have access to relevant information and users? Can they make the decisions they need to? Does the collaboration work? And does the technical foundation make it easy or hard to deliver?

A composite example

A team was under constant delivery pressure, and the intuitive solution was to add people. At the same time, several important decisions had to be cleared outside the team, and the developers were often waiting for answers before they could move on. More people would have meant more hands, but it would not have removed the waiting. Only when the team’s decision space became clearer did the flow improve.

Direction before activity

A team can deliver a lot without creating corresponding value. This tends to happen when the distance between business and technology grows, or when the team receives predefined tasks without understanding why they matter. The team can then become very efficient at building what someone has asked for, without necessarily finding out whether it is the right thing to build.

Good technology teams therefore need more than a backlog. They need an understanding of the problem, the effect the organisation wants to achieve and the boundaries they are working within. Within clear boundaries, they also need room to use their competence.

Both people and technology can create friction

A team’s capacity is not the sum of individual performances either. Knowledge can be shared or concentrated. Disagreement can make the solution better or make people more cautious. Experienced professionals can make others more independent, or create dependence around themselves.

In the same way, technical debt, manual processes, unclear ownership and many dependencies can eat up a large part of the capacity. More people is then not necessarily the best investment. Sometimes it matters more to remove the friction that is holding back the people who are already there.

Before you add people

Before the next developer joins the team, it can be useful to look at where the capacity actually goes. Does the team know what effect it is trying to create? Does it have real room to act? Do people make each other better? Does the technology support the work?

More people can mean more capacity. But if the system is what limits capacity, more people can also just mean more people running into the same limits.

Technology should create value

The aim should therefore not be to maximise the number of development hours, but to get as much value as possible out of technology investments. That requires looking at the problem to be solved, the people who will solve it, the room they have to act and the foundation they will build on.

The most important question is not how much technology we can produce, but what value the technology enables the organisation to create.
